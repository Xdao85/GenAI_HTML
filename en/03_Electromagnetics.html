Point Charges, Superposition and Waves at an Interface

A single point charge creates an electric field (EMF-E); several charges combine by superposition into patterns of field lines and equipotentials (EMF-M); and a one-dimensional finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulation sends a pulse onto a dielectric, where the measured reflection and transmission are checked against the Fresnel formulas (EMF-C).

Level E · EMF-E

Coulomb's Law and the Field of a Point Charge

A charge $q$ changes the space around it: any other charge brought nearby is pushed or pulled. The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ describes this influence at each point. It points radially away from $q$ (towards it if $q<0$) and weakens as $1/R^2$.
Field and force are different: the field exists at a point whether or not a charge is there, while the force $\mathbf{F}=q'\mathbf{E}$ appears only when a test charge $q'$ is placed there, and reverses if $q'<0$.

Coulomb's law

$$\mathbf{F}_{21}=\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{12}\,\frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon R_{12}^2}$$

The force on $q_2$ due to $q_1$; $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{12}$ points from $q_1$ to $q_2$. Like charges ($q_1q_2>0$) repel, unlike charges attract.

Electric field

$$\mathbf{E}=\hat{\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon R^2}\quad(\mathrm{V/m})$$

The force per unit test charge, $\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{F}/q'$. Doubling $R$ cuts it to a quarter.

Vector form

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{R})=\frac{q\,(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_1)}{4\pi\varepsilon|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_1|^3}$$

Use this form when the charge sits at $\mathbf{R}_1$, not at the origin. The numerator sets the direction; the cube leaves a $1/R^2$ magnitude.

Worked example

Take $q=2$ nC in air. At $R=0.3$ m the field is $|\mathbf{E}|=\dfrac{9\times10^{9}\cdot2\times10^{-9}}{0.09}=200$ V/m. A test charge $q'=-1$ nC there feels $0.2\ \mu$N. Since $q'<0$, the force points towards $q$, opposite to $\mathbf{E}$.

Field of positive and negative point charges

Line density shows $|\mathbf{E}|$. Every line leaving the charge crosses any sphere around it, so the count is the same at every radius while the area grows as $R^2$. That spreading is the inverse-square law.

Level M · EMF-M

Field Lines and Equipotentials of Point Charges

Each charge contributes its own Coulomb field, and the total field at any point is their vector sum. Drag the charges or the probe $P$. The background shows the electric potential $V$ (rose positive, sky negative); white curves are field lines from the positive charges. Compare the dipole with two like charges: between like charges the fields cancel at a null point. Changing $q$ scales the field, not the pattern.

Superposition at $P$

$$\mathbf{E}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{q_i(\mathbf{R}_P-\mathbf{R}_i)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\mathbf{R}_P-\mathbf{R}_i|^3}$$
$\mathbf{E}(P)$ (superposition)
$-\nabla V$ (numerical, central difference)
$V(P)$

The analytical sum of $\mathbf{E}$ and the numerical gradient of $V$ are independent calculations, yet they agree, because $\mathbf{E}=-\nabla V$: the field points where the potential falls fastest. Along an equipotential $V$ does not change, so $\mathbf{E}$ has no component there, and field lines cross equipotentials at right angles.

Level C · EMF-C

1D FDTD: A Pulse at a Dielectric Interface

When a wave meets a new material, part reflects and part passes on. A Gaussian pulse travels in air ($z<z_i$) into a lossless dielectric with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ ($z>z_i$). FDTD steps Maxwell's curl equations in time on a staggered Yee grid (Courant number 1); two probes record the incident, reflected and transmitted pulses, giving measured $\Gamma$ and $\tau$.
Raise $\varepsilon_r$: the reflected pulse grows and returns inverted, while the transmitted pulse slows and shortens. The same physics governs reflections at microwave substrates, radomes and optical coatings.

Validation: analytical ($\varepsilon=2\%$) + MATLAB

Fresnel reference (normal incidence)

$$\Gamma=\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1}{\eta_2+\eta_1}=\frac{1-n_2}{1+n_2},\qquad \tau=1+\Gamma=\frac{2}{1+n_2}$$
$$H_y^{q+\frac12}[m]=H_y^{q-\frac12}[m]+\frac{E_z^q[m+1]-E_z^q[m]}{\eta_0}$$$$E_z^{q+1}[m]=E_z^{q}[m]+\frac{\eta_0}{\varepsilon_r[m]}\,\Delta H_y^{q+\frac12}[m]$$

$\Delta H_y[m]=H_y[m]-H_y[m-1]$; Courant number $c\Delta t/\Delta z=1$.

$n_2=\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$
$\Gamma$ analytical · FDTD
$\tau$ analytical · FDTD
$E_{\mathrm{rel}}$ ($\Gamma$, $\tau$)
Cells per pulse width in medium 2
Energy check $\Gamma^2+n_2\tau^2$

For $n_2>1$, $\Gamma<0$ (inverted reflection) and $\tau<1$, yet energy is conserved: $\Gamma^2+n_2\tau^2=1$. In medium 2 the pulse is $n_2$ times shorter, so a narrow pulse spans only a few cells; numerical dispersion distorts it and the measured $\tau$ drifts. Reduce $w$ at $\varepsilon_r=12$ to see the validation fail, which is why FDTD is allowed $\varepsilon=2\%$.

MATLAB reference script (same Yee scheme)
N = 1400; zi = 700; src = 150; pA = 420; pB = 950;
er2 = 4; w = 25; t0 = 4*w; T = 2400; imp0 = 377;
ez = zeros(1,N); hy = zeros(1,N); eps = ones(1,N);
eps(zi+2:end) = er2; eps(zi+1) = (1+er2)/2;
A = zeros(1,T); B = zeros(1,T);
for q = 1:T
  hy(1:N-1) = hy(1:N-1) + diff(ez)/imp0;
  e1 = ez(2);
  ez(2:N) = ez(2:N) + diff(hy).*imp0./eps(2:N);
  ez(1) = e1;                          % Mur ABC (exact for S = 1)
  ez(src) = ez(src) + exp(-((q-1-t0)/w)^2);
  A(q) = ez(pA); B(q) = ez(pB);
end
n2 = sqrt(er2); Gamma_th = (1-n2)/(1+n2); tau_th = 2/(1+n2);
