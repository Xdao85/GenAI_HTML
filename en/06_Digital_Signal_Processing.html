DFT, Spectral Leakage and FIR Filter Design

Tasks DSP-E, DSP-M and DSP-C cover the core of every spectrum analyser: the DFT turns a finite record into frequency bins, the window decides whether a weak tone stays visible beside a strong one, and windowing an ideal impulse response yields an FIR filter whose measured gains you check against theory.

Level E · DSP-E

The DFT and Frequency Resolution

Bin $k$ compares the record with a sinusoid completing exactly $k$ cycles, so the $N$-point DFT samples the spectrum at $N$ equally spaced frequencies. The spacing $\Delta f=f_s/N=1/T$ depends only on the record duration $T$: only a longer record gives finer bins. Zero-padding merely interpolates the same spectrum; it does not improve resolution.

Definition

$$X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N}$$

Bin frequencies

$$f_k=k\frac{f_s}{N},\quad \Delta f=\frac{f_s}{N}=\frac1T$$

Symmetry (real $x$)

$$X[N-k]=X^*[k]$$

Upper bins mirror lower ones, so only bins $0\dots N/2$ (DC to $f_s/2$) carry independent information.

Example

$f_s=8$ kHz and $N=8$ give $\Delta f=1$ kHz. The signal $x[n]=\cos(2\pi\cdot2n/8)+\tfrac12\cos(2\pi\cdot3n/8)$ completes exactly 2 and 3 cycles, so energy appears only in bins 2 and 3 (and mirrors 6 and 5). Amplitude $A$ gives $|X[k]|=AN/2$: $|X[2]|=4$, $|X[3]|=2$.

Level M · DSP-M

Windowing and Spectral Leakage

The DFT treats the record as periodic. If a tone does not complete an integer number of cycles, the ends do not join and its energy leaks into all bins; a weak second tone can disappear under the sidelobes of the strong one. Tapered windows lower the sidelobes at the cost of a wider main lobe, which blurs close tones.

With tone 1 at 16.5 bins, switch from Rectangular to Blackman and watch tone 2 emerge; move tone 1 to an integer bin and leakage vanishes. This is how a small fault line is found beside a machine's running-speed peak.

Level C · DSP-C

Windowed-Sinc FIR Design and Filtering

The ideal low-pass impulse response $h_d[n]=2f_c/f_s\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(2f_c(n-\tfrac{M-1}{2})/f_s\right)$ is infinitely long, so it is truncated to $M$ taps and multiplied by a window: lower sidelobes give a deeper stopband, a wider main lobe a wider transition band. A high-pass filter is obtained by spectral inversion. The filter is applied to $x[n]=\sin(2\pi50n/f_s)+0.5\sin(2\pi300n/f_s)+$ noise, with $f_s=1$ kHz. Increase $M$ to sharpen the transition (and lengthen the delay), or move $f_c$ to see which tone survives.

Validation: analytical + MATLAB fir1/freqz

Measured: DFT of the steady-state output per tone; analytical: $|H(e^{j\omega})|=\left|\sum_n h[n]e^{-j\omega n}\right|$. Expect about −6 dB, not −3 dB, at $f_c$. Symmetric taps delay every frequency by $(M-1)/2$ samples, preserving waveform shape.

MATLAB reference script
fs = 1000; fc = 150; M = 61;
h = fir1(M-1, fc/(fs/2), 'low', hamming(M));
[H, f] = freqz(h, 1, 4096, fs);
plot(f, 20*log10(abs(H))); grid on
n = 0:fs-1;  x = sin(2*pi*50*n/fs) + 0.5*sin(2*pi*300*n/fs);
y = filter(h, 1, x);
abs(H(round(50/fs*2*4096)+1))   % compare with the in-page gain
